Figure 4. Simplified construction of the upper-left diagram on the plate marked “§ 28. page 68” in Discours du mouvement local (1670) by Ignace-Gaston Pardies, showing an oblique collision with a body initially at rest.

Obliquity:

A before impact (Aa) A after impact (aa) B after impact (Bb) lateral component (Ac) perpendicular component (ca) initial and final separation (AB, ab)

Reading the Figure

  1. Segments representing motion correspond to distances traversed in equal times and are therefore proportional to speeds.
  2. A moves along Aa and strikes B, which is at rest. At the moment of impact their centres are at a and B.
  3. A’s incoming motion resolves into the lateral Ac, parallel to the common tangent at contact, and the perpendicular ca, parallel to the line of centres.
  4. Under Pardies’s rule, A retains its lateral component and communicates its perpendicular component to B (§28): A moves to a (aa = Ac) and B to b (Bb = ca).
  5. Since Ac + ca > Aa, the sum of the bodies’ speeds, which Pardies treats as their “absolute motion” (§§29, 31), increases.
  6. For equal intervals before and after impact, the initial separation AB equals the final separation ab, illustrating the conservation of “respective motion” (§29).
  7. With Aa held fixed, increasingly grazing contact narrows the angle between Aa and Ac: Ac approaches Aa, while ca approaches zero. A therefore retains almost all its speed and changes direction only slightly.
  8. On Pardies’s understanding of indifference, bodies offer no intrinsic resistance to being set in motion, whatever their size. He therefore takes the rules to hold in vacuo, “whether the Bodies be Equal or not” (§31).
